A TikTok claim goes viral, and you have a deadline. The video is 70 seconds of fast speech over music, the captions vanish when you pause, and you need the exact words before you can check a single one of them.

This guide is the workflow for journalists, fact-checkers and OSINT researchers who work with TikTok regularly. It covers how to get the spoken words out as text, what to do with them, and what a transcript cannot prove.

## Why TikTok verification starts with the audio

**On TikTok, the claim is usually spoken, not written.** The caption under a video is often a few hashtags; the assertion you need to check is in the voice-over. You can't fact-check a claim you can't quote accurately.

TikTok itself won't give you that text. It shows auto-captions during playback but has no copy, export or download option for them anywhere in the app or on the web, and many creators never turn captions on. Typing out a minute of fast speech by hand is slow and introduces errors.

A transcript solves three problems at once:

- **Exact quotation.** You quote what was said, not your memory of it.
- **Search.** Names, figures and dates become findable with Ctrl+F.
- **Translation.** Text translates far more reliably than audio, which matters for foreign-language claims.

## The TikTok verification workflow

**Get the words first, then check the claims, then check the footage.** These steps follow the five questions Bellingcat's beginner's guide to social media verification puts to any post: originality, who, where, when and why.

### 1. Preserve the link and the moment

Copy the share link and note the date and time, the view count and the account handle. TikToks can be edited, made private or deleted. A downloaded transcript (TXT, DOC or PDF) is a record of what was said even if the video later disappears, though it is not a substitute for archiving the video itself.

### 2. Transcribe the spoken audio

1. Paste the link at [transcribetok.com](https://transcribetok.com) and click Get Transcript.
2. Read the full transcript in a few seconds. No account is needed to read it.
3. Create a free account to copy it, or to download it as TXT, DOC or PDF. Add timestamps so every quote can be matched to a moment in the video.

TranscribeTok transcribes the audio track, so it works on videos with no captions. It only reaches public videos, and it does not capture text typed on screen. [The complete guide to getting a TikTok transcript](/how-to-get-a-tiktok-transcript) covers accuracy limits and every format.

### 3. Pull out every checkable claim

Read the transcript with a highlighter. Mark each proper noun, number, place, date and attributed quote. Those are your checks. Everything else is opinion or framing. The [TikTok transcript with timestamps guide](/tiktok-transcript-with-timestamps) shows how to keep the time reference next to each line.

### 4. Trace the source account

Bellingcat's guide shows the method: find the original poster, then read their other uploads for context. A clip reposted with a sinister claim may come from an account with an innocent explanation in plain view.

This is where transcripts scale. Transcribe the poster's last 10 to 20 videos and search them for the same claim, the same names, or an earlier version of the story. If a claim first appears in a video three weeks before the viral one, you have found an earlier source. The [TikTok influencer vetting guide](/tiktok-influencer-vetting) uses the same 20-video method to audit creators before brand deals.

### 5. Check the sound, not just the speaker

**On TikTok, the audio may not belong to the person in the video.** Creators reuse sounds from other videos, so a voice-over can be lifted from an older or unrelated clip. Tap the sound name to see other videos using it, and transcribe the earliest one you can find. If the words match, the claim predates the video you are checking.

### 6. Check the visuals with visual tools

A transcript proves what was said, not what was filmed. For the footage, use keyframes and reverse image search. The free InVID-WeVerify browser plugin extracts keyframes, reads metadata, runs OCR on images and searches across networks including TikTok, with some features limited to registered users with a professional email address.

## What a TikTok transcript can and cannot prove

**A transcript is evidence of what was said in a video, nothing more.** Be precise about that line when you write it up.

| Question | Transcript answers it? | What to use instead or as well |
|---|---|---|
| What exactly was said? | **Yes**, word for word, with timestamps | Re-listen to confirm any line you quote |
| Is the spoken claim true? | No, but it lists the claims to check | Primary sources, official records, experts |
| Who said it first? | Partly, by searching earlier videos | Sound page, reverse search, other platforms |
| Where was it filmed? | Only if a place is named aloud | Geolocation of signs, landmarks, terrain |
| When was it filmed? | Only if a date is mentioned | Upload date, shadows, weather, events |
| Is the voice real? | **No** | Voice-clone and deepfake checks, the creator |
| What does on-screen text say? | **No**, overlays aren't captured | OCR on a keyframe |

Accuracy is in the mid-to-high 90s for clear speech and drops with loud music, sped-up audio, slang and code-switching. Before publishing a quote, play the moment back.

## TikTok's own fact-checking, briefly

**TikTok works with IFCN-accredited fact-checking organisations, and labels some claims it can't verify.** According to its transparency centre, a claim fact-checkers can't confirm may be labelled "unverified" and made ineligible for the For You feed. In July 2025 TikTok launched Footnotes in the US, a crowd-sourced feature where qualified users add context to videos; TikTok said it expands its fact-checking program rather than replacing it.

Neither gives you the text of a video. A label tells you a claim was disputed; a transcript lets you check it yourself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you verify a TikTok video from its transcript alone?**
No. A transcript tells you exactly what was said, which is what you fact-check, but it cannot show where or when the footage was filmed or whether it was edited. Pair the transcript with source tracing, reverse image search on keyframes, and geolocation of what is visible in the video.

**How do journalists get the text of a TikTok video?**
TikTok shows auto-captions while a video plays but offers no way to copy or export them. Journalists paste the video's share link into a transcript tool such as TranscribeTok, which transcribes the spoken audio of any public TikTok, including videos with no captions, in a few seconds.

**What is TikTok OSINT?**
TikTok OSINT is open-source intelligence gathered from public TikTok content: videos, sounds, profiles, comments and metadata. Journalists, fact-checkers and researchers use it to find the source of a claim, establish who posted footage first, and document what a creator has said publicly.

**Does TikTok fact-check videos itself?**
TikTok works with IFCN-accredited fact-checking organisations through its Global Fact-Checking Program. If a claim cannot be verified, TikTok says it may label the video "unverified" and make it ineligible for the For You feed. In the US it also runs Footnotes, a crowd-sourced context feature launched in July 2025.
