If you have gone looking for a way to download the captions from a TikTok video, you have probably already discovered the answer: there isn't one.

TikTok generates auto-captions, displays them during playback, and offers no way at all to get them out. No copy button, no export, no download, no select-all — not in the app, not on the web, not in the creator tools. The captions are rendered onto the video surface, which means they are pixels, not text.

Here is the workaround that actually produces a caption file.

## Why TikTok's captions can't be exported

Worth understanding, because it explains why every method that *sounds* like it should work doesn't.

TikTok's captions are a **playback feature**. They exist so viewers can follow along with the sound off. They were never built as a data export, and TikTok has no commercial reason to make video scripts easy to extract in bulk — it is the raw material of the platform.

Practical consequences:

- You cannot select the caption text with your cursor
- Screenshots give you an image, not text
- Even as the creator of the video, there is no caption export in the upload tools

So the only route to a caption file is to regenerate the text from the audio.

## What an SRT file is, and where to use it

An SRT is plain text with a specific structure — a sequence number, a timecode range, and the caption line:

```
1
00:00:00,000 --> 00:00:02,400
Here's the mistake almost everyone makes

2
00:00:02,400 --> 00:00:05,100
when they start posting on this app
```

It is the most widely supported subtitle format there is. You can upload one to YouTube, Instagram, LinkedIn or Vimeo, drop it into Premiere, Final Cut, DaVinci Resolve or CapCut, or attach it to a video file for accessibility.

This matters most when you are **reposting a TikTok elsewhere**. Cross-posting to YouTube Shorts or Reels with a proper caption track — rather than TikTok's burned-in captions with the watermark — looks better and is treated better by those platforms.

## One thing to check before you publish

Captions generated from audio will not match TikTok's caption track word for word. Both are automatic transcriptions of the same speech, made by different systems, so they diverge in predictable places:

- **Punctuation and line breaks** differ noticeably
- **Slang spelling** varies — one may write "gonna", the other "going to"
- **Names and brands** are the usual errors in both

For most uses this is irrelevant. If the captions are going onto a video you are publishing, read them through once. A 60-second clip takes under a minute to check, and the errors that matter — a mangled brand name, a wrong number — are obvious on a skim.
